### Electricity: how it costs money and still cannot strand you `#151` 📌  [decisions, design, power]
THE PROBLEM
Charging costs money. A player with no money and no charge cannot earn, and
cannot charge. That is a hard lock, and an incremental must not have one.

THE ANSWER — two parts that compose

1. THE TRICKLE SOCKET. A fixture, not a purchase: a slow mains point that is
   always free. It charges at roughly a fifth of a proper pad. It cannot strand
   you because it is always there and never billed — but it costs the one thing
   that is genuinely scarce in a day-based game, which is TIME. Standing at it
   eats the working day.

   This is also what makes the first purchase obviously worth making. The
   player does not need telling that a charge point is good; they can feel half
   their day going into the wall socket.

2. A TAB. Every commissioned pad bills by the unit drawn. If the money is not
   there, the charge still happens and the balance goes on account. Income
   clears the balance before it becomes profit.

   This is better than waiving the charge when broke, which sounds equivalent
   but is exploitable — a player who always spends to zero before charging gets
   free power forever. A tab is not exploitable, never refuses service, and is
   self-correcting: power is 10-20% of what a crate earns, so any productive
   work pays it down. The only way to grow the debt is to drive about not
   working, which is the player's choice and fixes itself the moment they stop.

WHY THIS IS WORTH THE COMPLEXITY

It is one meter for the whole game. The factory's electricity demand later
bills to the same account, as does anything else that draws power. Running the
place on credit is also a real thing that happens to real businesses, so it
needs no explaining, and it opens a whole later line — interest, credit limits,
paying the tab down for a reputation bonus — without inventing a new system.

WHAT THIS REPLACES
The earlier 'bill only when the money exists' rule. Same guarantee, no exploit.

### Upgrade rules — levels, pricing, and how the axes extend `#150` 📌  [design, upgrades]
LEVEL COUNTS

· Per-bot upgrades: 1-5 levels each, no more. Every level costs a trip to the
  shop with that specific bot, and that trip is the real price. A twenty-level
  per-bot upgrade would be twenty drives.
· Global upgrades: 25-100 levels. These are bought from anywhere in the shop,
  cost nothing but money, and are where the long tail of progression lives.

PRICING

Nothing maxes out in the phase that introduces it. Price curves are set so a
line stays worth buying into for a phase or two afterwards, which means at any
moment the player has more things worth buying than money to buy them with.
That is the choice: not which upgrades are available, but which order to take
them in. Nothing is ever locked out by taking another first.

THE TWO AXES MUST BE OPEN-ENDED

More sizes and more weight classes are coming, so neither axis can be a closed
enum of the three and two that exist now.

· SIZE as deck capacity in crate units: 1, 2, 4, ... A bot's deck holds a set of
  crates that fit. 'Can it take a 2x2' becomes 'does 4 units fit', and vertical
  extension later is just more units.
· WEIGHT as a rating compared against a crate's own weight class: timber 1,
  steel 2, and whatever comes after at 3, 4. A bot lifts anything rated at or
  below its own.

Written this way, adding a size or a material is data, not a code change.

WITHDRAWN FROM THE CATALOGUE

· 13 Twin deck — folded into SIZE. Vertical stacking is a later size step, not
  a separate upgrade that would fight it.
· 19 Loop orders — makes no sense once auto-pickup and auto-deliver exist. Its
  real home is the factory phase, and probably a different chassis: a bot whose
  job is a fixed circuit between machines rather than one that chooses work.
· 78 Battery health — only exists if capacity decays, and adding decay purely to
  sell the cure is a bad trade.

### Intro phase — spec `#141` 📌  [design, intro]
TIME
  1 real second = 1 in-game minute. The working day runs 07:00 to midnight —
  1,020 real seconds, seventeen minutes. Night is skipped or fast-forwarded.
  (Current code: dayLengthSeconds 240 for a full 24 h. Needs changing.)

  The long day is deliberate and stays long. It is thinking room: time to drive
  about, look at things, and later to pull an assembly line apart and rebuild it
  without a new quota landing every five minutes. It is not meant to be filled
  with work.

THE LOOP
  Right-click a crate, right-click the mark in the trailer, hit the plate to
  send the truck. Trucks do not leave on their own — that is an upgrade.

PACING — each unlock adds a source of action, and the player chooses the pace
  · First truck sent          -> the shop opens
  · First charge point bought -> the first boat is allowed to arrive
  · Then queue length, then per-bot automation, then a second bot
  Someone who wants to keep busy always has a new thing; someone who wants to
  potter about can.

WHAT THE INTRO DOES NOT DO
  It does not end with a clear floor. The 1x2s, the 2x2s and the steel all
  need upgrades that arrive later, so a good deal of cargo is still sitting
  there when the factory phase starts. That is intentional: you plan the first
  factory around the crates you cannot move yet, and clearing them later buys
  you floor space and a better layout. Floor space becomes a resource.

QUEUE
  Starts at length 0 — one order at a time. Bought up a step at a time, and
  deliberately NOT the first purchase: charging comes first, then queue, then
  per-bot automation, so the player buys a bot, automates it and watches it work.

  The queue must not become vestigial once bots are automatic. Its long-term
  home is player-authored routes in the factory phase — drawn paths through an
  assembly line, rather than bots only ever choosing for themselves.

CHARGING
  Every charge point sealed at the start. The first purchase is a charge point.
  Charging costs money by the amount drawn, subject to the no-lock rules.

### No game over — how the intro avoids a hard lock `#148` 📌  [design, intro]
The intro has to teach that power runs out and costs money, without ever
leaving a player unable to continue. The failure it must never allow: flat bot,
empty floor, no money.

Proposed rules, all cheap to implement:

· RESERVE FLOOR. A bot never drops fully to zero. It keeps a small reserve —
  enough to crawl and to reach a pad. Low power still refuses to lift, so the
  lesson lands, but the bot is never immobile.

· THE BOAT WAITS. No boat arrives until the first charge point is bought. You
  cannot be given more work than you have the power to do.

· CHARGING CANNOT BANKRUPT YOU. Power is billed by the amount drawn, but if you
  cannot pay, you get charged anyway and the meter simply does not run. The
  cost is real when you have money and invisible when you do not. This keeps the
  mechanic without ever being the thing that ends a run.

· MISSING A QUOTA COSTS SOMETHING, NOT EVERYTHING. A fine, a docked payment, a
  reputation hit. Never a run ending.

Together these mean there is no state the player can reach that has no way out,
which is what an incremental should guarantee. The pressure comes from wanting
to go faster, not from the threat of losing.

### Design feedback — what works and what I would watch `#142` 📌  [design, review]
WHAT IS STRONG

· Boat as faucet, truck as sink. This is a real two-sided loop and it finally
  gives the water dock a job. The starting mess being a one-off and the boat
  being the ongoing supply is a clean story: the intro is "clear the building",
  the game is "work the port".
· The shop as a physical room solves the per-bot upgrade targeting problem for
  free. The bot on the plate is the bot upgraded.
· Freezing both the clock AND the factory in the shop is the right call. The
  drive there and back is the cost.
· Queue starting at zero. Correct. Teach one thing at a time.

WHAT I WOULD WATCH

1. THE BIGGEST RISK: the least automated phase is first.
   Every incremental that front-loads manual work loses people in the first
   twenty minutes. The pleasure of the genre is watching the machine run, not
   being the machine. Auto-pickup should arrive far earlier than instinct says
   — inside the first hour of play, not as a mid-game reward.

2. Seventeen-minute days versus a twelve-crate quota.
   A round trip is about twenty seconds, so a day holds roughly fifty crates.
   A quota of twelve is done in four minutes, leaving thirteen minutes of
   nothing. Either the quota fills most of the day, or the early days are
   shorter. Dead air teaches the player the game is slow.

3. The floor runs out before the boats arrive.
   Twenty-nine liftable crates on the floor, twenty-four slots in a trailer.
   Day one at quota twelve leaves seventeen; day two clears it. Boats need to
   start by day two or three, not after the intro ends. Introducing the water
   early is also just better — it is the most distinctive thing in the building.

4. One hard loss in a seventeen-minute intro is expensive.
   Consider making the FIRST miss scripted and survivable (a fine, a warning, a
   docked payment) with the real game-over on the second, even in the intro.
   The lesson lands either way; the restart does not cost seventeen minutes.

5. Tune the electricity price deliberately.
   A crate costs 0.0245 of a charge and sells for $45. For power to be a real
   but not crushing cost — say 10-20% of revenue — a full charge should be
   roughly $200-360. Worth choosing rather than discovering.

6. Speed upgrades do not reduce energy per crate, and that is good.
   Drain is per DISTANCE, not per second, so going faster costs the same charge.
   Route length and efficiency are the energy levers; speed is the time lever.
   Two clean, separate axes. Keep it that way.

7. Make the quota typed from day one.
   Even while it is always "12 timber crates", store it as a list of
   (item, count). Retrofitting item types into a crate counter later is
   expensive; designing it in now is free.

8. Per-bot upgrades get awkward around a dozen bots.
   "New bots ship with what you own" needs to arrive before the fleet is big
   enough for shopping trips to be a chore. A retrofit-all option too.

9. Night is free tension.
   The board going amber as the day runs out is a deadline signal that costs no
   new systems and uses the day cycle you already have.

### Game shape — the whole arc `#140` 📌  [design, vision]
The game is a long chain of phases. Each one shifts what the player is
managing, while keeping everything from the previous phase alive to go back and
optimise. Nothing is ever taken away; every phase is more advanced than the last.

1. INTRO — clear the abandoned warehouse.
   One manual robot, crates all over the floor, a daily quota. Teaches movement,
   the order queue, that cargo differs, the battery, and selling. Ends when the
   floor is clear.

2. THE PORT — boats supply, trucks ship.
   A flatbed boat berths between the indents in the water dock and extends
   boarding platforms. Bots board it and take crates off. It leaves when it is
   empty and no bot is aboard; a new one comes later. Fast work means more boats
   per day. This is what replaces the finite starting stock, and it is the first
   ongoing loop.

3. THE FACTORY — open crates for components, assemble goods, sell for more.
   Forced by the quota starting to demand built items rather than raw crates.

4. MULTIPLE SITES — buy adjacent warehouses, bots travel between them, sites
   supply each other.

5. BEYOND — seafloor, space. Thousands of bots.

6. PRESTIGE — not designed yet.

The through-line is bots: getting them, upgrading them, and eventually watching
them work without you.
